Poem From the Inside of a Chinatown Laundromat

today in a laundromat I will be more beautiful than yesterday, ad for detergent-bleached skin and my tightest slutty dress. listening to my ex-boyfriend's favorite songs. tossing out my best suit. my grease-stained pants. pockets fat and guilt-ridden. today, the man I was seeing says he cannot love me, still takes my best nudes in mood lighting. says he admires the way I inhale other women every lipstick stain begins the same— crumpling in a girl's teeth crimson, dying handsomely. what's left: her mouth, loving the sun into the shape of a goodbye, the first girl to wake on her bedsheets, dew or something like dawn cresting on her hips. fingers scattered, like these stars. today, I wanted to be small and enough to make love to without the lights off. again and again, skin against skin. myself only when I'm closest to magazine-cover idols, girls of nineteen, gratuitous pornography. against all odds, wear everything you once found beautiful. I'm sorry. I've tried to be better, wiped the countertops clean, cracks forming all maps, following the GPS back home. I do the laundry in smaller swathes of technicolor. the way I wear my bruised knees— pain as a calendar of memory. a man's voice, that fucker. I had once begged for his lie. even spread eagle, I will not learn to forget every minor road: a lover from your side, leaving like myself. that I leave all the taps open, that I do not exist unless I am clean. meaning the man wants to fuck me even still. meaning there's so many ways to still love me. in another life, I will pour gold stars down my throat. dust across locked cabinets, upturned tables, bolted to everything that hurts you. remember the dream where a woman dressed my body, tenderly, or with some alien form of knowing
in america I was alive. at least enough to witness the falling of snow. in this country, I dream of a day spent skipping rocks and stockpiling bottles, the rims still slick with foreign spit. wearing this flag like a bulletproof vest. this town's heat, like lightning— named god and his gruesome city that turns, everything I own against me. even my own reflection on linoleum, flowers as the first point of contact. in the laundromat a bullet blooms. coins jingle in your hands. praying to older temples, all devotees dead on sight. when thirsty, I swallowed my hometown's night, called it a prayer for rain, scooping water from the sound and let it rush between my porous skin. learnt forms of an embrace, trying to be something skinner—a leaf does not forget the hunger of its branches so easily. now flashes echo. another bag unpacking itself. I call it laundry day, how I cannot help my love for extinction. today, I'm so full of nostalgia. summers without gunfire, american-made denim. spent the day skipping rocks. every good problem begins in california. out for years, all we could eat were maggots. other vermin. stomach your city, stolen from memory like a love song. picture this: my ancestors wear bullet holes to welcome the new year. a street-side vendor dresses in red, misses from our city corner. so long, mother tongue, saliva-slick. I am no prey. I do not feather so easily. where I'm from, a missed step can so easily turn laughter into shrapnel. I cannot help that I eat my sadness like sandwiches. in these times, do whatever it takes to stay full, listening to the president on the radio. swallow the contrary voice: my parents dream of success. self-made hemorrhage. blanketing the skyline. my mother came gently down the stairs, saying she's made it, finally made it how I love America, crushed coke bottles, its open wounds, questioning why everything I've loved is lined with soap.
