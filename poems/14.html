March Madness or Being a Baller!

Beginning like it always begins, underneath a reddening moon. The sky, filled with smoke, mutes birdsong to silence. Strange, that is only when we are eating stale bread and hard cheese when you ask me to be exclusive. I drop my apple (this is not a metaphor) on the leaf litter; bark and tiny ants cling to the bitten surface. Shrugging, I go in for another bite anyways—the flavor of dirt and sugar fills my mouth, forms a film over my tongue. A frog hops onto your shoulder; I take this as a sign.

Okay, I said. Let's be exclusive.

We had met on Hinge a few months prior. After a litany of coffee dates, museum visits, and walks in the park, I had almost given up and deleted the app. Well, I wouldn't—the faces were so inviting, the screen so unimaginably bright when sitting in the dark. It is a basic human instinct to be beholden to light, no matter the artificiality of its origin. I swipe and swipe and swipe till my vision blurs, till the phone slips out of my grip, the screen protector shattering on the hardwood.

When I saw you, or rather your profile, I didn't think much of it. That's how busy I was those days, so busy getting my bag up. Those days I felt like the city pulled me every which way like that medival torture method where you tied each limb to a different horse and sent them galloping in different directions except my right arm was drawn to North Beach, my left to the Sunset, and my right leg somewhere else, somewhere I don't even know, maybe homeward, maybe towards that trail of smoke, darting towards the sky.

You told me we should go hiking on our next date because you could tell that I was a girl who spent too much time on her phone. That's how we ended up here, some small coastal town in NorCal, a few hours north of SF, at your suggestion. In hindsight though, maybe you just wanted to question me about why I went on a date with your ex-roommate and didn't tell you, had to hear from a friend of a friend of a friend. In my defense, you were, and still are my favorite one, all the other ones like songs on the radio—beautiful for the moment.

In the distance, I hear the sirens. Some dumbass camper, you said earlier in the night, probably lost control of their campfire. I laugh, though worried, the closest I've gotten to an open flame being my apartment's electric stove that I rarely use, partly out of laziness, partly out of fear. Somewhere, a branch snaps. I bristle. The woods rustle around us, as if fleeing.

We are not fleeing. We are making out in the deergrass. For a moment, I mistook its long tendrils for the strands of your hair, grasping it tight enough for the blades to slice through the meat of my palm. We pause long enough in our making out to somehow acquire me a band-aid but not long enough to apply it properly. In the frenzy, it slips off, my open wound smearing blood on the log we are sitting on.

I've never felt this way with anyone else. It's cliche, but true. The night ends. It's soon, but the timing's right. I think I might love you.

Truthfully, this is the story that I wanted to tell you before I had ghosted. Maybe if I had sent this message by passenger pigeon it would be better, or by good ol' snail mail, or if I had bothered sending it at all. Maybe a text would've sufficed but in this day and age, what do we owe anyone? Not anything, really.

And truthfully, I barely think about you at all, now nine months after that date, now that the sky swells with so much daylight, closing the curtains before I have a chance to see the sun setting. How could I, in this economy? It's only when I root through my fridge that I remember that night, when my hand finds its way to an apple. I grip it tight and my fingers puncture through its flesh. When held in front of the fluorescence of the fridge lighting, I spot the colony of mold resting on its shriveled skin. I imagine each little spot calling out EAT ME, EAT ME, to let those spores linger in every cell of my body and have its spindly branches web out, enveloping me and the floor, hardwood or tree lichen.

Yet starvation is an act of efficiency; the apple lands in the trash. Its juices cling to my skin, creating a greasy film. I picture 95 calories gone from the day, shaving off ten minutes from the gym, my next vacation post where I will be so goddamn skinny, so goddamn beautiful. Only then, rearranged in a permutation of pixels, will I be loved.

Lighting a cigarette, I let the air fill with smoke. The embers hit the concrete of my balcony, then dissipate. It's been a long time since I've sat with the night. Instead of 99.7's TOP BAY HITS FOR EARLY MORNING, I turn the radio dial to catch the static, hoping that there could be some clarity gleaned from the airwaves. There's a certain frequency only created by the sound of rubber scraping against the interstate, of the mounting billboards blocking the sweet horizon, in every photo where there's a body that's been buried, shelved, cached, or archived.

When you are gone, or more like now you are gone, who will dry my hair off, fold the towels in perfect pastel rows, then gently tiptoe into our bedroom, lift our covers, and sneak into bed? And who will quiet the engine roar, to veer the car off the road, if only to barely miss the deer caught in its lights?
